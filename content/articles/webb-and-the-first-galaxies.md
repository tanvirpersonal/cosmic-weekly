The James Webb Space Telescope was built to answer a deceptively simple question: what did the first galaxies look like? Looking far away means looking back in time, and Webb was designed to look back further than any telescope before it.

## Why infrared

Light from the earliest galaxies has travelled for more than thirteen billion years. During that journey the expansion of the universe stretched it, shifting visible light into the **infrared**. Webb's instruments are built for exactly that part of the spectrum, which is why the telescope carries a gold-coated mirror and a tennis-court-sized sunshield.

## Built to stay cold

Infrared telescopes must be extremely cold, otherwise their own heat drowns out faint signals. Webb launched on 25 December 2021 and settled near a point called **L2**, about 1.5 million kilometres from Earth, where the Sun, Earth and Moon all sit in roughly the same direction. The sunshield keeps the instruments in permanent shadow.

| Feature | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| Primary mirror | 6.5 metres across, 18 hexagonal segments |
| Observes | Mostly near- and mid-infrared light |
| Location | Sun–Earth L2 point |

## What it is finding

Webb has revealed galaxies that existed only a few hundred million years after the Big Bang. Some appear brighter and more developed than many models expected. That does not break cosmology, but it forces astronomers to revisit how quickly stars and galaxies could form in the early universe.

> Every new telescope answers an old question and quietly hands us a better one.

## The slow part

Measuring a galaxy's true distance requires a spectrum, not just a bright dot in an image. Much of the real work now is careful follow-up, one galaxy at a time.
