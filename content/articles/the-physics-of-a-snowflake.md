A snowflake is one of the few places where microscopic physics becomes visible to the naked eye. Its six-fold shape is a direct signature of how water molecules like to arrange themselves.

## Why six

When water freezes at normal pressure, its molecules link together through hydrogen bonds into a **hexagonal lattice**. That internal geometry is built in, and it shows up on the outside: ice crystals grow with six sides or six arms.

## Why every arm matches

A snowflake falls through the air as one small crystal. Each of its six arms passes through nearly the same temperature and humidity at the same moment, so each grows in nearly the same way. The result is a pattern that looks planned, although nothing is directing it.

> Symmetry here is not design. It is six arms reading the same weather.

## Why no two are alike

The path through the cloud is never identical. A small difference in humidity or temperature changes how the branches form, and those differences accumulate. The shape of a finished flake is a record of the journey it took.

## What shapes the shape

Researchers have mapped how crystal form depends on conditions:

- **Plates and stars** tend to form at certain temperatures.
- **Needles and columns** form at others.
- **More moisture** generally means more elaborate branching.

## A short history of looking

In 1885, Wilson Bentley, a farmer in Vermont, took the first photomicrograph of a single snowflake. He went on to photograph thousands, and his images helped turn a passing curiosity into a subject of scientific study.
