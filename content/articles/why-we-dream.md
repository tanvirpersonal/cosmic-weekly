Every night, for years of our lives, we watch strange films that nobody else can see. Scientists still do not agree on exactly why.

## Sleep has a rhythm

Sleep is not one long state. It moves through cycles of roughly ninety minutes, alternating between deeper, slower sleep and a lighter, more active phase called **REM** (rapid eye movement). REM was identified in 1953 by Eugene Aserinsky and Nathaniel Kleitman, and for decades it was treated as the stage of dreaming.

We now know that dreams can happen in other stages too. They tend to be most vivid and story-like in REM.

## The main ideas

No single theory has won. Several are taken seriously:

- **Memory consolidation:** the sleeping brain replays and reorganises the day's experiences, strengthening some and letting others fade.
- **Emotional processing:** dreams may help soften the sting of difficult memories by revisiting them in a safe setting.
- **Rehearsal:** some researchers suggest dreams let the brain practise responses to threats and social situations.
- **Byproduct:** others argue dreams are simply what a busy, self-maintaining brain feels like from the inside.

> A good theory of dreaming has to explain the memory, the emotion and the oddness all at once.

## What is reasonably clear

Sleep matters for learning and mood. People who are deprived of sleep struggle to form and recall memories, and their emotional reactions become harder to regulate.

## A small practice

Keeping a notebook by the bed and writing a few lines on waking is a simple way to notice how your own mind behaves at night, without needing a theory at all.
