We live surrounded by fast answers. A question appears, and within seconds there is a confident reply. That speed is useful, but it quietly changes what we expect thinking to feel like.

## Some ideas need time

An idea that is truly new rarely arrives fully formed. It usually begins as a vague discomfort: something does not quite fit. Giving that feeling room, without rushing to resolve it, is often where the interesting thinking happens.

> Understanding is not the same as having an answer. It is what is left after you have stayed with a question long enough.

## Three habits worth trying

1. **Write before you search.** Put your own half-formed answer on paper first. You will notice exactly where your understanding runs out.
2. **Walk without input.** Many writers and scientists have described their best ideas arriving during unhurried walks. No headphones, no feed.
3. **Return to the same question.** Revisit a hard problem on different days. The version of you that comes back is not quite the one who left.

## Slow is not the same as lazy

Slow thinking is not about doing less. It is about choosing where depth is worth the effort, and being willing to say "I don't know yet" while you work it out.
