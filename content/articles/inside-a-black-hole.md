Some places in the universe are so extreme that our best theories stop agreeing with each other. A black hole is the clearest example: a region where gravity is strong enough that nothing, not even light, can climb back out.

## The point of no return

The boundary of a black hole is called the **event horizon**. It is not a surface you could touch. It is a line in spacetime: cross it, and every possible future path leads inward. To a distant observer, an object falling toward the horizon appears to slow down and fade. For the object itself, nothing special happens at the moment of crossing.

> A black hole is not a hole in space. It is a place where space has been bent so far that "out" is no longer a direction.

## What we have actually seen

For most of the twentieth century black holes were a mathematical consequence of Einstein's general relativity. That changed within a decade:

- In **2015**, the LIGO detectors recorded gravitational waves from two black holes merging.
- In **2019**, the Event Horizon Telescope released the first image of a black hole's shadow, in the galaxy M87.
- In **2022**, the same team imaged the black hole at the centre of our own Milky Way, known as Sagittarius A*.

## The singularity problem

General relativity predicts that matter inside a black hole collapses toward a point of infinite density, called a singularity. Most physicists read that as a warning sign rather than a fact: the equations are being pushed beyond where they can be trusted.

Stephen Hawking showed in 1974 that quantum effects make black holes glow very faintly and slowly evaporate. That result opened a puzzle, the *information paradox*, which is still unresolved.

## Why it matters

Black holes are where gravity and quantum mechanics collide. Whatever theory eventually joins the two will have to explain what really happens past the horizon.
